Kinusaiga vs Kimekomi: What’s the Difference?

Both tuck fabric into grooves. One starts as a flat board. The other starts as a form with depth.

Kinusaiga, on these pages, is flat fabric inlay on scored foam. You finish a picture you can frame and hang. Kimekomi is typically three-dimensional. The form might be carved wood, such as a doll, or a padded form. The cloth follows that volume.

The shared gesture is a groove that hides a fabric edge. The split is what you cut the groove into, and what you hold when you stop. If you want a wall picture and you do not want to carve or pad a form, you want kinusaiga. Start here.

Flat or three-dimensional

Look at the object. The shared word is “tuck,” and that word does not tell the crafts apart. A flat board with a picture on one face is the kinusaiga project this site teaches. A form that already has depth, before any cloth goes on, is the usual kimekomi project. English pages sometimes use the two words as if they were the same. Follow the materials list in front of you when a title and a photo disagree.

Practical difference for a first project. On a narrow screen, scroll sideways.
Kinusaiga Kimekomi
Shape Flat. Little depth. One face carries the picture. Typically three-dimensional. A padded form or a carved form has volume.
Support Foam you can score with a craft knife. About 1 inch of firm XPS for a first piece. Often carved wood, such as a doll. A padded form is the other common support.
What you finish A picture to frame and hang. The back is the back of the foam. The covered form. Grooves travel over curves.
First afternoon A 6 to 8 inch leaf with large lobes. A couple of hours. You need the form before the cloth. This site does not teach you to carve or pad one.

A person can tuck cloth into a foam ball and still be doing a cousin of both crafts. That is not the first project here. A 6 to 8 inch foam offcut will not become a doll, and a flat-picture supply list will not become a padded form.

Materials

Kinusaiga asks for a foam board, a double-sided adhesive sheet or tape on the face, a sharp craft knife, the flat end of a seam ripper, and medium-weight cotton. The score is about 1/4 inch deep. Regular display foam board is usually too thin. Those judgments are on Foam and supplies.

Kimekomi asks for the form first. Traditionally that is carved wood. A padded form is the other typical start: you build the volume, then cut grooves along the design so the cloth can follow a shoulder, a sleeve, or a curve. The tools overlap in spirit. Something sharp cuts a line. Something blunt pushes cloth. The base is not a flat practice board.

Do not buy the kinusaiga list and hope it answers the carved or padded project. Do not buy a doll-form list and hope it answers the flat leaf. Artist credit, if you need it while you sort the names, stays cautious: Japanese artist, often credited as Maeno Setsu (also seen as Maeno Takashi) in the 1980s. Sources disagree. That sentence is not a claim that one person invented both crafts.

Which one is harder first

Start with kinusaiga if you want a fabric picture on the wall. The hard parts are local and visible: a groove that is too shallow, a dull blade that crushes the foam, a shape cut to its outline before it is tucked. You can see each one on a flat board and correct it. The beginners guide is that session. Keep the drawing large. A stem and four lobes on a 6 to 8 inch board is enough.

Kinusaiga gets harder when the shapes shrink. Thin strips, lettering, and sharp points tear the foam and leave no cloth to tuck. That is a later piece, not a better first one.

Kimekomi is the harder first project because the difficulty starts before the tuck. You have to carve wood or build a padded form, then run grooves over a volume so the cloth stays smooth on a curve. A mistake in the form is still there after the cloth goes on. Nothing on this path teaches that carving or that padding. If the photo you were watching is a doll or a rounded padded shape, this is the wrong first teacher.

Learning one does not ban the other. The first afternoon should still pick one object. Pick the flat leaf.

What you need for the flat picture

  • About 1 inch of XPS foam, trimmed to about 6 to 8 inches.
  • A double-sided adhesive sheet, or double-sided tape used on the face only.
  • A sharp craft knife and the flat end of a seam ripper.
  • Medium-weight cotton, with about 1/2 inch of spare cloth past each line.
  • A leaf you draw: stem and four lobes. The sequence is First pattern.

Silk is not required. A frame can wait until the tucks stay down. Choosing a box versus separate pieces is Kit vs materials, and that page is only about the flat picture.
