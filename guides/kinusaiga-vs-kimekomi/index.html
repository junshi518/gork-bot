Kinusaiga vs Kimekomi: What’s the Difference?

Both tuck fabric into cut grooves. They usually start from different bases and finish as different objects.

Kinusaiga and kimekomi both tuck fabric into cut grooves, but they are not the same craft in practice. Kinusaiga is usually a flat picture on a foam board, made as no-sew wall art. Kimekomi more often covers a carved wooden form, such as a doll. If you want a first fabric picture to hang on a wall, you are looking for kinusaiga.

English pages sometimes use the two words as if they were identical, and some makers tuck cloth into foam forms that are not flat. This page describes the usual difference so a beginner can tell which project they meant to start. It is not a rule that one person can never do both, and it is not a history verdict. Public sources already disagree on names; this draft does not add a new one.

The short difference

Look at the object, not only at the verb “tuck.” If the support is a flat board and the result is a picture, the pages on this site call it kinusaiga. If the support is a shaped form, often carved wood, and the cloth is covering that form, the usual name in this draft is kimekomi. The shared idea is a groove that hides a fabric edge. The split is what the groove is cut into, and what you hold when you are done.

That split is “usual” and “often,” not “always.” A person can tuck fabric into a foam ball, or into a wooden panel, and still be doing a cousin of both crafts. If a tutorial’s title and its materials list disagree, follow the materials list.

Materials and base

Kinusaiga, in the draft these pages are reviewing, asks for a foam board you can score with a craft knife. The working thickness is about 1 inch. A double-sided adhesive sheet holds the field of each fabric shape, and a shallow groove, about 1/4 inch, hides the edge. Regular display foam board is usually too thin for that groove. The full comparison of boards, cloth, and tools is on the foam and supplies page, which is the materials reference for every guide here.

Kimekomi, in the usual sense, asks for a form that already has shape before any cloth is applied. Traditionally that form is carved wood. Grooves are cut along the design on that form, and fabric is pushed into them so the shape stays rounded or figured under the cloth. The tools overlap in spirit — something sharp to cut a line, something blunt to push cloth — but the base is not a flat practice board, and a 6–8 inch foam offcut will not become a carved doll.

A shopping list for one base is a poor list for the other. Foam, a sheet of adhesive, and quilting cotton answer a kinusaiga first piece. They do not answer how to carve a form. This preview does not sell either kind of supply.

Artist credit, if you need it while you are sorting names, stays in the cautious wording used on the beginners page: Japanese artist, often credited as Maeno Setsu (also seen as Maeno Takashi) in the 1980s. Sources disagree. That sentence is not a claim that one person invented both crafts, and this page does not pick a single spelling as fact.

What you make

A finished kinusaiga piece from this draft is a flat picture. You can lean it, frame it, or hang it. The design is on one face. The back is the back of the foam. Nothing about the method requires the board to become a body, a ball, or a box. If your drawing has large shapes and few sharp corners, you are set up for the first session the beginners guide describes.

A finished kimekomi piece, in the usual sense, is the form you covered. People often meet it as a doll. The cloth follows curves. Grooves travel over a volume, not across a single plane. That is a different set of problems: how the form was carved, how the grooves meet at a shoulder or a sleeve, how the cloth is kept smooth over a curve. This batch does not teach those problems, and it does not include patterns for them.

Learning one does not ban the other. The first afternoon should still pick one object. There is no free pattern library here for either craft. A first kinusaiga drawing can be one you make: a circle, a simple house, or a few large leaves.

Which should a beginner start with?

If you want a fabric picture on the wall, and you do not want to sew, start with kinusaiga. The beginners guide is the page that chooses the first board and the first drawing. Use a 6–8 inch board, large shapes, and few sharp corners. Plan on a couple of hours for that simple piece, as a planning range, not as a clock we have verified with photos.

If you want a doll or another carved form, this site is the wrong first teacher. Do not buy a flat-picture foam list and hope it becomes that form. Do not treat the shop placeholder as a kimekomi catalog. It has three kinusaiga categories, and even those are not orderable.

If you are still unsure which word you searched, use the table above on the object in the tutorial you were watching. Flat board and a picture: stay here. Carved form: leave this batch for a source that actually teaches that form. The kit vs materials page is only about gathering supplies for the flat picture.

What you need if you choose kinusaiga

The materials are the same as any first kinusaiga piece, and they are specified on the supplies page rather than repeated as a second full guide. Short version: about 1-inch XPS foam, a double-sided adhesive sheet, a sharp craft knife, a blunt tucking tool, medium-weight cotton, and a simple pattern you draw. Silk is not required. Display foam board is usually too thin.

Choosing a box versus separate pieces is a different question from choosing kinusaiga versus kimekomi. Once you know you want the flat picture, use kit vs materials for that decision.
