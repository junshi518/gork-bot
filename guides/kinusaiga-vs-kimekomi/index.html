Kinusaiga vs Kimekomi: What’s the Difference?

Both tuck fabric into grooves. One starts as a flat board. The other starts as a form with depth.

Kinusaiga, on these pages, is flat fabric inlay on scored foam. You finish a picture you can frame and hang. Kimekomi is typically three-dimensional. The form might be carved wood, such as a doll, or a padded form. The cloth follows that volume.

The shared gesture is a groove that hides a fabric edge. The split is what you cut the groove into, and what you hold when you stop. If you want a wall picture and you do not want to carve or pad a form, you want kinusaiga. Start here.

Flat or three-dimensional

Look at the object. The shared word is “tuck,” and that word does not tell the crafts apart. A flat board with a picture on one face is the kinusaiga project this site teaches. A form that already has depth, before any cloth goes on, is the usual kimekomi project. English pages sometimes use the two words as if they were the same. Follow the materials list in front of you when a title and a photo disagree.

A person can tuck cloth into a foam ball and still be doing a cousin of both crafts. That is not the first project here. A 6 to 8 inch foam offcut will not become a doll, and a flat-picture supply list will not become a padded form.

What you are holding at the end

A kinusaiga piece from this path is a flat board. The design is on one face. The back is the back of the foam. You can lean it, frame it, or hang the frame. The method does not turn the board into a body, a ball, or a box. The first drawing is a stem and four lobes, each lobe at least about 1 inch across, on a board about 6 to 8 inches, with about 1 inch of bare foam around the picture.

A kimekomi piece, in the usual sense, is the form you covered. People often meet it as a doll. The cloth follows curves. Grooves run over a volume: a shoulder, a sleeve, the turn of a padded cheek. The problems start with the form. How was the wood carved, or how was the padding built? How do the grooves meet on that curve? How does the cloth stay smooth where the surface bends? This page does not teach those problems.

Use the side view as the quick test. Kinusaiga has little depth. The picture sits on one plane. Kimekomi has depth before any cloth is applied. The schematic on this page draws the same leaf both ways so the support is the only change. If the object already has a rounded body, you are not holding the flat project, and a foam offcut will not grow that body.

Materials

Kinusaiga asks for a foam board, a double-sided adhesive sheet or tape on the face, a sharp craft knife, the flat end of a seam ripper, and medium-weight cotton. The score is about 1/4 inch deep. Regular display foam board is usually too thin. Those judgments are on Foam and supplies.

Kimekomi asks for the form first. Traditionally that is carved wood. A padded form is the other typical start: you build the volume, then cut grooves along the design so the cloth can follow a shoulder, a sleeve, or a curve. The tools overlap in spirit. Something sharp cuts a line. Something blunt pushes cloth. The base is not a flat practice board.

For the flat picture the numbers are specific, and they are enough to start without opening another tab. The board is about 1 inch of XPS. Regular display foam board is usually too thin. The score is about 1/4 inch deep and stops in the foam. Adhesive or tape sits on the face and stays out of the groove. The tucking tool is the flat back of a seam ripper, not the forked point and not a ball tip. Medium-weight cotton is enough, with about 1/2 inch of spare cloth past each line. Silk is not required. Those supplies answer the flat leaf. They do not answer how to carve wood or how to pad a form.

Kimekomi still needs something sharp to cut a line and something blunt to push cloth. The overlap stops there. You cannot score a shoulder into a 6 to 8 inch offcut. You cannot pad a doll from a double-sided sheet. If the lesson in front of you starts with a carving gouge, a wooden body, or a form you stuff and stitch, you are in the other craft even if the title says kinusaiga.

Do not buy the kinusaiga list and hope it answers the carved or padded project. Do not buy a doll-form list and hope it answers the flat leaf. Artist credit, if you need it while you sort the names, stays cautious: Japanese artist, often credited as Maeno Setsu (also seen as Maeno Takashi) in the 1980s. Sources disagree. That sentence is not a claim that one person invented both crafts.

Which one is harder first

Start with kinusaiga if you want a fabric picture on the wall. The hard parts are local and visible: a groove that is too shallow, a dull blade that crushes the foam, a shape cut to its outline before it is tucked. You can see each one on a flat board and correct it. The beginners guide is that session. Keep each lobe at least about 1 inch across. A stem and four lobes on a 6 to 8 inch board is enough for a couple of hours.

Kinusaiga gets harder when the shapes shrink below about 1 inch, when the lines become lettering, or when a corner comes to a point. Thin strips tear the foam and leave no cloth to tuck. That is a later piece. It is not a better first one, and it is not a reason to switch to a three-dimensional form.

Kimekomi is the harder first project because the difficulty starts before the tuck. You have to carve wood or build a padded form, then run grooves over a volume so the cloth stays smooth on a curve. A mistake in the form is still there after the cloth goes on. A shallow groove on a flat board can be scored again. A shoulder carved in the wrong place cannot. Nothing on this path teaches that carving or that padding. If the photo you were watching is a doll or a rounded padded shape, this is the wrong first teacher.

Learning one does not ban the other. The first afternoon should still pick one object. Pick the flat leaf. You can come back to a form after the tuck on a board is familiar.

How to choose this afternoon

You want a picture on the wall, and you do not want to sew. Choose kinusaiga. Use about 1 inch of XPS, adhesive or tape on the face, a sharp knife, the flat end of a seam ripper, and cotton. Draw the leaf on the pattern page, then score it in the beginners session. When an edge pops, the cause is on the board in front of you: depth, a crushed wall, or cloth with no margin.

You want a doll, or a padded form you can turn in your hands. Choose a teacher who builds that form. A flat foam list will not become it. The carving or the padding is the work that comes before any tuck, and it is why kimekomi is the harder start. Do not buy the kinusaiga categories and rename them.

You are unsure which word you searched. Look at the object in the lesson you were watching, then use the table on this page. Flat board and a picture: stay here. Carved wood or a padded form: leave this path. If the title says one craft and the materials say the other, follow the materials. A title is not a board.

What you need for the flat picture

  • About 1 inch of XPS foam, trimmed to about 6 to 8 inches.
  • A double-sided adhesive sheet, or double-sided tape used on the face only.
  • A sharp craft knife and the flat end of a seam ripper.
  • Medium-weight cotton, with about 1/2 inch of spare cloth past each line.
  • A leaf you draw: stem and four lobes. The sequence is First pattern.

Silk is not required. A frame can wait until the tucks stay down. Choosing a box versus separate pieces is Kit vs materials, and that page is only about the flat picture.
