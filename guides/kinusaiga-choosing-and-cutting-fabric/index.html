Cloth

How to Choose and Cut Fabric for Kinusaiga

Medium-weight cotton, a print you can see past, and about 1/2 inch of cloth past every groove.

The groove is about 1/4 inch deep. The cloth has to fold into that slot and lie flat on the adhesive. Quilting cotton, or a similar medium-weight cotton, does that on a first piece. Silk is not required. Do not delay the leaf to find silk.

Cut each piece larger than its shape. Tuck it. Then trim the tail. Cutting the finished silhouette first removes the only cloth the groove can hold.

Weight

Hold the cloth. If it folds over your finger without standing up like a card, and it is not so thin that you can see your hand through a light color, it is in the right range. Quilting cotton sits in that range.

  • Use it: quilting cotton, or another medium-weight cotton, in solids or calm prints.
  • Skip it for the first piece: heavy wool coating. It will not enter a 1/4 inch groove. You will crush the foam trying.
  • Skip it for the first piece: slippery lining. It slides on the adhesive and will not stay where you pressed it.
  • Skip the rule that says you must use silk. Cotton teaches the tuck.

Wash and dry new cotton if it might shrink. Then press it. A hard crease across a lobe shows forever once the adhesive grabs the middle. Frayed threads along a cut edge are normal. They go into the groove. Long loose threads on the face do not. Pick them off before you press the cloth down.

Print scale

The lobe is the unit. Compare the print to that lobe, not to the whole board.

  • A solid shows every ridge. Use at least one solid, ideally the background, so you can see the tuck.
  • A sparse print, with shapes smaller than the lobe, is fine. You can still see whether the edge went in.
  • A print larger than the lobe gets cut in half and looks accidental. Save it for a later, larger shape.
  • A tiny dense print can look “finished” while the tuck is still sitting on top of the line. Avoid it until you trust the low-angle check on the beginners page.

You need one piece for the background, one for each of the four lobes, and one for the stem. Six pieces, each cut as a rough rectangle, not as a finished leaf part.

Grain

The selvage is the finished edge of the cloth. Straight grain runs parallel to the selvage. Cross grain runs from selvage to selvage. Bias is the diagonal. Bias stretches.

On a first piece, keep every piece on the straight grain, and keep that grain running the same way on the board: up and down. The stem’s long direction should follow the straight grain, so the strip does not grow longer as you push it. A lobe cut on the bias wrinkles when you push the edge, because the cloth stretches sideways into the groove.

You do not need to pull a thread to test this. Fold the cloth. The fold that lies flat, without a twist, is a grain line. The fold that ripples is closer to the bias. Cut the rectangles on the flat fold.

What you need

  • Six pieces of medium-weight cotton, pressed, large enough to cover each shape with spare cloth.
  • Fabric scissors. The craft knife is for foam. A knife on cloth, over the board, can slice the groove wider.
  • The flat end of the seam ripper, for the trial tuck before you trim.
  • The scored board, with liner still on the shapes you are not working yet.

Materials

Cotton is usually already at home. Foam, adhesive, and tools are the shop categories if they are not: Foam and adhesive, Tools, Starter kit.

The margin

Leave about 1/2 inch of cloth past the groove on every side. That is enough to enter a 1/4 inch slot and stay there. Less than that, and the edge pops out because there is nothing in the channel. Much more than that, and you are stuffing a roll of cloth on top of the line.

The 1/2 inch is a rough cut, not a traced seam allowance you measure with a gauge on every curve. Eyeball it. The trial tuck tells you if you were short.

Cut after a trial tuck

This is the order. Reversing it is the mistake the next figure shows.

  1. Rough-cut a rectangle bigger than the shape, with about 1/2 inch to spare on every side. Do not follow the curve of the lobe with the scissors yet.
  2. Peel the liner for that one shape. Lay the rectangle on it, grain running up and down. Press the middle so the adhesive holds the field.
  3. With the flat end of the seam ripper, push the edge into the groove along one side. If the cloth will not stay, stop cutting. The groove is too shallow, or the walls are crushed. Fix that on the troubleshooting page before you trim anything.
  4. When the edge stays down, trim only the tail that will not fit. On a curve, trim a little, tuck, and trim again. On a corner, tuck both sides up to the meeting point first. If a lump remains, snip into the margin toward the corner and stop before the groove. Then tuck the flaps.
  5. Move to the next shape. Where two colors share a groove, the first edge is already in. Tuck the new edge beside it. Do not pull the first one out to “neaten” the pair.

If you already cut a lobe to its finished outline, that scrap cannot be saved by pushing harder. Cut a new rectangle from the same cloth and start that shape again. The background is the easiest place to get this wrong, because the field is large and the border is long. Leave the 1/2 inch there too.

The full tuck order for the leaf is background, lobes, stem, then the border. It is written as a session on the beginners page.

Next
