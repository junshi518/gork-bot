What Foam Do You Use for Kinusaiga?

The board, the sheet that holds the cloth, the knife, the tucking tool, and the fabric. No product is for sale on this page.

Most beginner kinusaiga is done on about 1-inch XPS insulation foam, covered with a double-sided adhesive sheet, then scored about 1/4 inch deep. Regular display foam board is usually too thin and soft to hold a tuck. You also need a sharp craft knife, a tucking tool such as the flat back of a seam ripper, quilting-cotton scraps, and a simple printed pattern.

Those sentences are the working answer in this draft. This project has no foam cross-section photo yet, and no supplier sheet has been accepted as a product we can list. The figures on this page are schematics, not photos. If a detail below cannot be checked on a real offcut, treat it as pending hands-on verification rather than as a lab result.

The short answer

For a first flat picture, look for a firm foam about 1 inch thick, specifically XPS (extruded polystyrene) of the kind sold as insulation board. Cover the working face with a double-sided adhesive sheet. Score the pattern about 1/4 inch deep, which is deep enough to swallow a cotton edge and shallow enough, on a 1-inch board, to leave foam under the cut.

Regular display foam board — the thin, often paper-faced sheet used for school presentations — is usually too thin for that slot. A knife either skims the surface, so the tuck pops out, or it passes through, so the board falls apart. Skip it for the first piece even if it is the foam you already have in a cupboard.

The rest of the kit is ordinary: a sharp knife, a blunt tool to push cloth, cotton scraps, and a pattern you draw. The beginners guide explains why that pattern should stay large and simple. This page stays with the materials.

Foam types compared

Thickness and firmness matter more than the color of the sheet or the picture on the package. The groove has to accept a folded edge of cotton. If the board is only a few millimeters thick, there is nowhere for that edge to go. If the foam is soft and crumbly, the walls of the groove collapse and the cloth will not stay down.

Board What to look for On a first tuck

Regular display foam board A thin sheet, often with a paper face, meant for signs and presentations. Usually too thin and too soft to hold a tuck. Skip it for the first piece.
Unlabeled craft foam or a kit board Type and thickness are unknown until you measure the actual sheet and read what the foam is. Do not assume it matches the 1-inch XPS figure because a listing says “foam.”

XPS is named here because a firm, closed surface takes a clean score. A soft open crumb crushes under the same knife. This draft does not rank brands, and it does not say every insulation sheet in a shop is the right thickness. Measure. About 1 inch is the figure. A board that is far thicker still works as a practice block, but it is heavier to hang later. A board that is far thinner cannot host the 1/4 inch groove this method uses.

A paper face can tear away from a thin core, so the groove’s walls are a film rather than a slot. If a knife already broke through a display board, that sheet was the wrong support. The first-piece guide assumes the thicker foam from the first cut.

Adhesive, knife, and tucking tool

The adhesive in this draft is a double-sided sheet on the foam face, not a puddle of wet glue painted over the picture. The sheet has a liner. You leave the liner on while you score, then peel one shape at a time as you lay fabric. Wet glue soaks cotton and can show through a light color. It is not the method listed here. If you try it anyway, you are outside this draft, and this page will not claim a result for it.

The sheet should cover the whole working face, including a small margin around the drawing. Gaps in the adhesive become bubbles in large shapes, because the tuck only captures the edge. Press the sheet down firmly when you apply it, and keep the liner clean. Dust on the adhesive is permanent once the cloth goes down.

The knife is a sharp craft knife or a snap-off blade. A serrated kitchen knife crushes XPS instead of parting it. Change the blade as soon as the cut looks torn or dusty rather than smooth. Score about 1/4 inch, once, along the line. Several timid scratches do not add up to a slot; they make a fuzzy trench that will not grip.

Cut away from the hand that holds the board. A 1/4 inch groove does not require a heavy lean. If you have to force the knife, the blade is dull or you are trying to cut too deep in one angry pass. A cutting mat protects the table and shows you when the knife has gone through, which it should not do on a 1-inch board.

The tucking tool only needs a blunt, narrow tip that can push cloth into the groove without slicing it. The flat back of a seam ripper is the stand-in named in this draft because many people already own one. A blunt plastic modeling tool can do the same job. The sharp point of a ripper, a pin, or the corner of the knife will cut the fabric or widen the groove until the edge will not stay. Use the flat end, and push along the line in short moves.

You do not need a special brand of any of these tools for a first piece. You do need them to be the right kind: sheet adhesive, a sharp thin blade, and a blunt pusher. The kit vs materials page is where that list turns into a buy-or-gather decision. This page does not claim a tool we sell.

Fabric that tucks well

Quilting cotton, or a similar medium-weight cotton, is the fabric this draft names for a first piece. It folds into a 1/4 inch groove without much bulk, and the adhesive can hold it flat. Solids and sparse prints make it easier to see whether an edge is hiding. A busy print can look finished while a tuck is still sitting on top of the line.

Silk is not required. Do not delay a first piece to find silk, and do not treat silk as a rule of the craft. Very thick cloth — a heavy wool coating, for example — is hard to push into the groove this method cuts. Very slippery lining slides on the adhesive and refuses to stay where you pressed it. Either can be a later experiment. Neither belongs in the first session.

Leave extra fabric past every line. The extra is the tuck. If you cut each shape to the finished outline before it touches the board, you have removed the only part the groove can hold. The beginners guide spends time on that mistake because it ruins an otherwise correct foam board.

Wash and dry new cotton if it might shrink, and press out a hard crease before you tuck. A crease that runs across a shape will show forever under the adhesive. Frayed threads along your cutting line are normal; they go into the groove. Long loose threads on the face are not. Pick them off before you press the cloth down, or they become part of the picture.

Large shapes need a tuck margin but an obvious amount of cloth. Tiny shapes burn margin on every piece, which is another reason a first design should stay simple. This preview does not include patterns or a yardage chart, and it does not promise a library of them.

Where a starter set would come from

A starter set, if one is ever confirmed, would have to match the figures on this page: foam about 1 inch thick and firm, a double-sided adhesive sheet, and a way to tuck. A box that only says “foam” without a thickness is not yet a match. A box that is a thin display board is the thing this page tells you to skip.
