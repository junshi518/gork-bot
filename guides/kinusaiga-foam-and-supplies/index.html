Materials

What Foam Do You Use for Kinusaiga?

A firm board about 1 inch thick, adhesive on the face, a sharp knife, and the flat end of a seam ripper.

For a first flat picture, use XPS foam about 1 inch thick. XPS is extruded polystyrene, the firm insulation board, not a crumbly bead foam. Cover the face with a double-sided adhesive sheet, or with double-sided tape if you cannot find a sheet. Score the pattern about 1/4 inch deep. Tuck with the flat back of a seam ripper.

Regular display foam board is usually too thin and too soft. Skip it even if it is already in the cupboard. A knife either skims the surface, so the tuck pops out, or it passes through, so the board falls apart.

Which foam

Thickness and firmness matter more than the color of the sheet. The groove has to accept a folded edge of cotton and still leave foam under the cut. Measure the sheet. About 1 inch is the figure used in the beginners session.

Regular display foam board A thin sheet, often with a paper face, meant for signs and school presentations. No. It is usually too thin. The same 1/4 inch score passes through.

A board that is a little thicker than 1 inch still works as a practice block. It is heavier to hang later, and the frame has to be deep enough for it. A board that is far thinner cannot host the groove this method uses. A paper face on a thin core tears away, so the “walls” of the groove are a film. If a knife already broke through a display board, that sheet was the wrong support. Do not score the same lines again hoping the second pass will grow more foam.

Measure the edge with a ruler before you trust the package. About 1 inch is the reading you want. Then pinch a corner you can trim off later. XPS dents and still cuts as a solid. Bead foam breaks into pellets in your fingers. A skin of paper that lifts off a thin core is display board, even when the wrapper says craft foam. The word “foam” is not a thickness.

Cut the practice piece down to about 6 to 8 inches on the long side, with square corners. The offcut is the place for a test score. A much larger first picture multiplies every shallow groove. A much smaller one hides whether a lobe of about 1 inch actually entered the slot. Keep the spare sheet until that test cut looks like a slot, not a scratch and not a hole through the back.

What you need

  • XPS about 1 inch thick, large enough to trim to a 6 to 8 inch board.
  • A double-sided adhesive sheet sized to that face, or double-sided tape for the same face.
  • A sharp craft knife or a snap-off blade, plus a spare blade.
  • A cutting mat.
  • A seam ripper you can turn to the flat end.
  • Medium-weight cotton. Detail is on Choosing and cutting fabric.
  • Paper, a pencil, and tape to hold the pattern. The drawing is on First pattern.

Adhesive or tape

Both go on the foam face. Neither goes in the groove. The tuck is what hides the edge. Adhesive in the channel gums the cloth and holds it up.

A double-sided sheet is the better default. It covers a whole face in one layer, and the liner lets you peel one shape at a time after you score. Press the sheet down firmly. Dust on the adhesive stays in the picture.

Double-sided tape is the stand-in when you cannot find a sheet. Score first, then lay tape on the face of one shape. Keep every strip out of the cut. Overlap the strips slightly on a large background, or the cloth will bubble in the gaps. Expose one shape at a time so the rest of the board stays clean.

Cover the whole face, including the bare margin around the drawing. A hole in the adhesive becomes a bubble, because the tuck only holds the edge. Press a sheet from the center outward before any liner comes off. Leave that liner on while the pattern paper is taped down and while you score. Peel one shape when you are ready to cover it, and peel only inside its grooves. The channel stays empty. If the whole liner comes off at the start, dust and loose threads stick to adhesive you are not ready to hide.

Tape misses the middle more easily than a sheet does, because it is strips. Run the strips across the field, overlap them, and stop at the lip of the groove. Press the center of the background after the cloth goes down. If it lifts between strips, you left a bare path. Add another strip on the next shape before you repeat that gap. Do not push tape down into the cut to “seal” an edge. The tuck is the seal.

Skip wet glue for this piece. It soaks cotton and can show through a light color. A pale background will show a wet spot you cannot tuck away. The sheet or the tape is the method.

The knife

Use a sharp craft knife or a snap-off blade. A serrated kitchen knife crushes XPS instead of parting it. Change the blade as soon as the cut looks torn or dusty rather than smooth.

Score about 1/4 inch, once, along the line. Several light scratches do not add up to a slot. Cut away from the hand that holds the board. A 1/4 inch groove does not need a heavy lean. If you have to force the knife, the blade is dull or you are trying to cut too deep in one angry pass.

The cutting mat protects the table and shows you when the knife has gone through. On a 1 inch board it should not go through. Test this in the margin before you score the leaf.

A usable groove has two walls you can see into, about 1/4 inch down, with foam still under the cut. The lips are smooth, not mashed, and the trench is not fuzzy. If the first pass stops short of that depth, deepen the same line with a sharp blade. Five timid scratches do not add up to a slot. They make a ragged trench that lets the cotton pop back out.

Set a spare blade on the table before the leaf starts. The moment a cut throws dust, or the foam looks torn instead of parted, swap the blade. Keep the hand that holds the board behind the knife. A 1/4 inch score is a steady pull. If you have to lean your weight on the handle, the blade is dull or you are trying to cut the board in half.

The tucking tool

You need a blunt, narrow end that can push cloth into the groove without slicing it. The flat back of a seam ripper is the household tool. Some rippers have a forked point on one end and a ball on the other, with a flatter span between them. Use that flat end. The flat back.

Do not tuck with the forked point. It cuts the fabric or widens the groove until the edge will not stay. Do not tuck with a ball tip. This method is not a ball-end method. A pin, or the corner of the knife, does the same damage as the point.

Push along the line in short moves. If the cloth bunches, back up and feed a smaller bite. A blunt plastic modeling tool can do the same job if its end is flat and narrow rather than a ball. You do not need a special brand for the first piece.

Find that flat end before any cloth is on the board. The forked point is the sharp end that picks stitches. Some rippers put a ball on the opposite end. Between them, or on the back of the handle, is the flat span. Turn that flat back into the groove. If the only ends you can find are a fork and a ball, do not invent a ball-end method. Pick a different blunt tool with a flat, narrow tip, and practice one push in the margin groove.

Sawing the tool back and forth packs cloth on top of the line. Move a short distance, lift, and move again. The edge should stay down when the tool leaves it. If it springs up, the groove is too shallow or the walls are crushed. Change the blade or deepen the score. Do not press harder with the wrong end of the ripper.

Cloth for this board

Quilting cotton, or a similar medium-weight cotton, is the cloth that matches a 1/4 inch groove. It folds in without much bulk, and the adhesive can hold the middle flat. Silk is not required. Do not delay the leaf while you look for silk.

Use a solid, or a sparse print, for the first picture. A solid background shows a ridge as soon as the tuck sits on top of the line. A busy print can look finished while the edge is still loose. Save a large-scale print for a later shape that is big enough to hold the motif.

Heavy coating, such as a wool meant for a jacket, will not enter this slot. You will crush the foam trying. Slippery lining slides on the adhesive and will not stay where you pressed it. Either cloth can be a later experiment. Neither belongs in the first session.

Wash and dry new cotton if it might shrink, then press it. A hard crease across a lobe stays visible once the adhesive grabs the field. Frayed threads along a cut edge are normal. They go into the groove. Long threads on the face do not. Pick them off before you press the cloth down, or they become part of the picture.

Leave about 1/2 inch of cloth past every line. That spare is what you tuck. If you scissor a lobe to its finished outline first, the groove has nothing to hold. On the drawing, each lobe should be at least about 1 inch across. Smaller shapes hide whether the trouble is the cloth or the slot. The order of cutting, after a trial tuck, is on the fabric page. The choice of cloth is settled here.

What to skip

  • Display foam board and crumbly bead foam.
  • Wet glue, and any adhesive placed inside the groove.
  • A dull blade you plan to “make work” by pressing harder.
  • The forked point of the seam ripper, and a ball tip.
  • Silk as a requirement. Cotton is enough to learn the tuck.
  • Heavy coating and slippery lining. One is too thick for a 1/4 inch groove. The other slides on the adhesive.
  • Tiny flowers, lettering, and strips narrower than the stem. Each first-piece lobe should be at least about 1 inch across. The drawing is on First pattern.

Skipping an item on that list is a decision you can make at the table. A thin board, a dull blade, wet glue in the groove, or cloth cut with no margin will fail the same way on the second try. Change the supply. Do not repeat the pass.
