Materials

What Foam Do You Use for Kinusaiga?

A firm board about 1 inch thick, adhesive on the face, a sharp knife, and the flat end of a seam ripper.

For a first flat picture, use XPS foam about 1 inch thick. XPS is extruded polystyrene, the firm insulation board, not a crumbly bead foam. Cover the face with a double-sided adhesive sheet, or with double-sided tape if you cannot find a sheet. Score the pattern about 1/4 inch deep. Tuck with the flat back of a seam ripper.

Regular display foam board is usually too thin and too soft. Skip it even if it is already in the cupboard. A knife either skims the surface, so the tuck pops out, or it passes through, so the board falls apart.

Which foam

Thickness and firmness matter more than the color of the sheet. The groove has to accept a folded edge of cotton and still leave foam under the cut. Measure the sheet. About 1 inch is the figure used in the beginners session.

Foam for a first piece. On a narrow screen, scroll sideways.
Board What you are holding Use it for the first tuck?
XPS insulation foam About 1 inch thick. Firm extruded polystyrene. It cuts in a clean slot instead of falling into pellets. Yes. This is the beginner board. A 1/4 inch groove still has foam underneath.
Regular display foam board A thin sheet, often with a paper face, meant for signs and school presentations. No. It is usually too thin. The same 1/4 inch score passes through.
Bead foam The white crumbly kind that breaks into pellets when you pinch an edge. No. The walls of the groove collapse, and the cloth will not stay down.
A board from a kit, type unstated You will not know until you measure the sheet and read what the foam is. Only after it measures about 1 inch and cuts like XPS. The word “foam” on a box is not a measurement.

A board that is a little thicker than 1 inch still works as a practice block. It is heavier to hang later, and the frame has to be deep enough for it. A board that is far thinner cannot host the groove this method uses. A paper face on a thin core tears away, so the “walls” of the groove are a film. If a knife already broke through a display board, that sheet was the wrong support. Do not score the same lines again hoping the second pass will grow more foam.

What you need

  • XPS about 1 inch thick, large enough to trim to a 6 to 8 inch board.
  • A double-sided adhesive sheet sized to that face, or double-sided tape for the same face.
  • A sharp craft knife or a snap-off blade, plus a spare blade.
  • A cutting mat.
  • A seam ripper you can turn to the flat end.
  • Medium-weight cotton. Detail is on Choosing and cutting fabric.
  • Paper, a pencil, and tape to hold the pattern. The drawing is on First pattern.

Adhesive or tape

Both go on the foam face. Neither goes in the groove. The tuck is what hides the edge. Adhesive in the channel gums the cloth and holds it up.

A double-sided sheet is the better default. It covers a whole face in one layer, and the liner lets you peel one shape at a time after you score. Press the sheet down firmly. Dust on the adhesive stays in the picture.

Double-sided tape is the stand-in when you cannot find a sheet. Score first, then lay tape on the face of one shape. Keep every strip out of the cut. Overlap the strips slightly on a large background, or the cloth will bubble in the gaps. Expose one shape at a time so the rest of the board stays clean.

Skip wet glue for this piece. It soaks cotton and can show through a light color. The sheet or the tape is the method.

The knife

Use a sharp craft knife or a snap-off blade. A serrated kitchen knife crushes XPS instead of parting it. Change the blade as soon as the cut looks torn or dusty rather than smooth.

Score about 1/4 inch, once, along the line. Several light scratches do not add up to a slot. Cut away from the hand that holds the board. A 1/4 inch groove does not need a heavy lean. If you have to force the knife, the blade is dull or you are trying to cut too deep in one angry pass.

The cutting mat protects the table and shows you when the knife has gone through. On a 1 inch board it should not go through. Test this in the margin before you score the leaf.

The tucking tool

You need a blunt, narrow end that can push cloth into the groove without slicing it. The flat back of a seam ripper is the household tool. Some rippers have a forked point on one end and a ball on the other, with a flatter span between them. Use that flat end. The flat back.

Do not tuck with the forked point. It cuts the fabric or widens the groove until the edge will not stay. Do not tuck with a ball tip. This method is not a ball-end method. A pin, or the corner of the knife, does the same damage as the point.

Push along the line in short moves. If the cloth bunches, back up and feed a smaller bite. A blunt plastic modeling tool can do the same job if its end is flat and narrow rather than a ball. You do not need a special brand for the first piece.

What to skip

  • Display foam board and crumbly bead foam.
  • Wet glue, and any adhesive placed inside the groove.
  • A dull blade you plan to “make work” by pressing harder.
  • The forked point of the seam ripper, and a ball tip.
  • Silk as a requirement. Cotton is enough to learn the tuck.
  • Heavy coating and slippery lining. One is too thick for a 1/4 inch groove. The other slides on the adhesive.
  • Tiny flowers, lettering, and strips narrower than the stem on the first pattern. Those drawings hide a bad groove.

Quilting cotton, or a similar medium-weight cotton, folds into the groove without much bulk. Solids and sparse prints make a ridge easier to see. Wash new cotton if it might shrink, and press out a hard crease before you tuck. How to cut the margin is on the fabric page, not here.
