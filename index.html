Kinusaiga for Beginners: No‑Sew Japanese Fabric Art

A flat picture made by tucking fabric into grooves scored in foam. This page is the path for a first piece.

What kinusaiga is

Kinusaiga is flat fabric inlay on scored foam. You draw a few large shapes, cut a groove along each line, and push the raw edge of the cloth into that groove. From the front, the colors meet. From the side, the support is a foam board, not carved wood and not a stuffed form.

The ordinary picture method does not sew the cloth to the board. A double-sided adhesive sheet, or double-sided tape, holds the middle of each shape. The adhesive stays on the face. It does not go in the groove. The tuck hides the cut edge.

People mix the name up with kimekomi. Both crafts tuck cloth into grooves. Kinusaiga, as these pages teach it, is a flat wall picture. Kimekomi is typically three-dimensional, for example a padded form or a carved wooden doll. If you want the flat picture, stay on this path. If you are not sure which object you meant, read Kinusaiga vs kimekomi before you buy a board.

When a name is required, the credit used here is cautious: Japanese artist, often credited as Maeno Setsu (also seen as Maeno Takashi) in the 1980s. Sources disagree, so this site does not pick one name as fact.

What to make first

Make one small leaf. Use a board about 6 to 8 inches on the long side, cut from about 1 inch of XPS foam. Score the lines about 1/4 inch deep. Cover the face with a double-sided adhesive sheet, and tuck with the flat end of a seam ripper. Use the flat back. Do not use the forked point, and do not use a ball tip.

The drawing is a stem and four rounded lobes, with a bare foam margin around them. Large shapes show you whether the edge actually went into the foam. A fine drawing hides that. Regular display foam board is usually too thin for the 1/4 inch groove, so do not start on the sheet from a school poster.

Medium-weight cotton is enough. Silk is not required. Plan on a couple of hours of quiet work for this one piece. Framing comes after the tucks stay down.

Learning path

Follow the list in order if you are new. If you already have a firm 1 inch board and cotton scraps, you can start at the pattern and come back to supplies only when a choice is unclear.

    Flat foam picture, or a three-dimensional form. Pick the flat picture before you gather tools.

  2. Foam and supplies

    Which board, which adhesive, which knife, and which end of the seam ripper. What to skip.

  3. Kit vs materials

    When one box is the simpler list, and when scraps you already own make a kit redundant.

  4. First pattern

    Draw the stem and four lobes, then tape the paper down and score through it.

  5. Choosing and cutting fabric

    Cotton weight, print scale, grain, and a 1/2 inch margin. Cut the silhouette only after a trial tuck.

  6. Beginners: the first session

    Preparation, scoring, adhesive, tuck order, the last side, and how to tell the piece is done.

  7. Finishing and framing

    Clean the edges, set the board in a deep enough frame, and hang the frame.

  8. Troubleshooting

    A shallow groove, a popped edge, wrinkles, corners, adhesive showing, and crushed foam.

Where to go next

If a 1 inch XPS board, a sharp craft knife, and cotton are already on the table, open First pattern and draw the leaf. Then follow the beginners session from preparation through the last tuck.

If you are still holding a thin poster board, or you are not sure which end of the seam ripper pushes the cloth, read Foam and supplies first. The wrong board wastes the afternoon.

If an edge pops up while you work, do not keep forcing it. Open Troubleshooting, fix the groove, and come back to the same board if the walls are still intact.
